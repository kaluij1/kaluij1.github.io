      What friction adds

      Without friction, air slips past the cylinder and closes up behind it, front and back mirror each other, and the pressures cancel. The predicted drag is exactly zero, which no one has ever measured. With friction, a thin layer of air sticks to the surface, cannot follow the curve round the back, and breaks away. The two breakaway layers roll up into vortices that leave alternately from the top and the bottom.

      That one change produces a wake, real drag, and a side force that swings back and forth at a steady rate. It is why wires hum in the wind and why chimneys and bridge cables can shake. The rate follows one number: frequency × diameter ÷ wind speed, the Strouhal number, which stays near 0.2 over a huge range of sizes and speeds.

      How far to trust it

      The shedding frequency lands within about 5% of wind-tunnel measurements from Reynolds 60 to 200. The drag coefficient reads about 10 to 15% above published values, mostly because the cylinder is only 10 grid cells across so that the solver can run live. Below about Reynolds 47 the wake correctly stays steady. Colours are blended between grid cells, and the smoke lines are drawn by carrying markers along the computed velocity.

      In the ideal-flow mode, temperature and pressure change the forces but never the streamlines, and drag is always zero. Every step for both models is worked out on the math page.
